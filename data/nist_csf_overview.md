# NIST Cybersecurity Framework Overview

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) is a voluntary set of guidelines from the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology. Organizations use it to manage cybersecurity risk in a structured, common language that works across industries.

## The five core functions

CSF organizes cybersecurity work into five core functions:

1. **Identify** — Know what assets, data, and systems you have, and understand the risks that threaten them. You cannot protect what you have not inventoried.
2. **Protect** — Put safeguards in place: access control, awareness training, data security, and protective technology.
3. **Detect** — Continuously monitor for anomalies and events so you notice incidents quickly.
4. **Respond** — Plan and execute response when an incident occurs: contain impact, communicate, and analyze what happened.
5. **Recover** — Restore capabilities and services after an incident, and improve resilience based on lessons learned.

These functions are not a strict sequence. Mature programs run Identify and Protect continuously, while Detect, Respond, and Recover stay ready.

## Profiles and tiers

An **organizational profile** describes current and target cybersecurity posture against CSF outcomes. **Implementation tiers** (Partial, Risk Informed, Repeatable, Adaptive) describe how deeply risk management is integrated into the organization — not a maturity score for every control.

## Why it matters for practitioners

CSF is widely referenced in job descriptions, audits, and vendor questionnaires. Mapping your controls to Identify–Protect–Detect–Respond–Recover helps you explain security work to non-specialists and find gaps. Version 2.0 expanded governance and supply-chain emphasis, but the five-function model remains the everyday vocabulary.

## Practical tip

When you describe a security project, say which CSF function it supports. Example: "This phishing detector supports Detect and Protect." That framing is clearer than listing tools alone.
